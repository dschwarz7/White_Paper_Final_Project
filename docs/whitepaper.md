## 1. Executive Summary {#executive}

The UCSB Library is a central study space for students, but difficulty finding suitable seating can make study sessions more stressful and less productive. In a survey of 46 UCSB students conducted for this project, 89.1% identified unavailable seating as a barrier to library use, and 80.4% reported leaving because they could not find a seat at least some of the time. Students also expressed preferences for quiet spaces and more accessible information about seating, reservations, and operating hours.

This white paper draws on library research from the University of Central Florida, East Carolina University, University College London, and Sam Houston State University. Based on this evidence and student feedback, the project recommends testing more efficient seating layouts while preserving quiet areas, evaluating more detailed occupancy tracking, and creating a centralized UCSB Library app that brings together study room reservations, library information, and seat availability. A phased pilot would allow UCSB to evaluate benefits, costs, privacy, and feasibility before expanding the changes.

## 2. Introduction & Context {#introduction}

Navigating college can easily be an overwhelming experience. Students have to navigate different clubs, events, social gatherings, and, on top of all this, actual schoolwork. One of the most stressful times in college revolves around midterm and finals season. A student who stops at the library between classes to either complete some work or study for a test may spend some of that study time searching for a seat. The last thing a student wants is to spend fifteen or more minutes looking for a spot and not even finding one, having to pivot and study somewhere else. Or, groups that may have finally found a time where everyone can meet may arrive and find that all study rooms have already been booked. Overcrowding is certainly not a new issue, but still a prominent one.

Overcrowding at the UCSB Library brings two related questions to mind: how much study space is actually available, and does that space meet the needs of students? A student prepping for an exam may just need a quiet desk, while a group of classmates working together through a problem set may need a space where they can converse with each other. An open seat on a quiet floor may help the first student but won’t do much for the group. Library hours, room reservations, and uncomfortable temperatures can further narrow students’ options by affecting when they can use the space and how long they can comfortably stay there.

This white paper examines how overcrowding affects UCSB students’ ability to find suitable study space at the UCSB Library. It considers whether adjusting the balance of quiet and collaborative spaces could better serve students during busy periods. It also explores how a centralized library app could help students reserve a study room more easily, check seating availability, and confirm operating hours before making a trip to the library. Alongside these proposals, the paper looks into possible temperature control methods and explores how other universities and programs have combatted this issue. The ultimate goal is to identify practical ways for UCSB to make its existing study spaces easier to find and use when students need them most. Problems with the UCSB Library


## 3. Current Challenges at the UCSB Library {#challenges}
# Overcrowding and Limited Study Space 

UCSB faces one significant issue dealing with overcrowding in the UCSB Library during finals and midterm weeks. UCSB has one campus library, and students rely on its resources to prepare for exams. During exam weeks, finding a seat becomes challenging due to the increased usage for studying and getting work completed. Students may end up spending more time trying to find a seat rather than completing the task they went in with. This can be very frustrating for students who have limited time between their responsibilities and classes.

One of the most significant challenges that students face at the UCSB Library is the lack of available spaces to study during academic periods. To understand the difficulties, we conducted a survey with 46 students attending UCSB and asked them to share their experiences with using the library. As shown in Figure 1, 89.1% of participants chose that unavailable seating was the highest barrier to using the Library, making it the highest reported issue. Other barriers include distance 54.3%, noise 39.1%, ventilation/air conditioning 23.9% and limited hours 4.3%. The results suggest that seating availability is the most prominent issue among participants, which can limit their ability to find a suitable area to complete their tasks at the library.

# Limited Library Hours

Another main concern is the UCSB Library hours. Every student's schedule varies with classes, employment, and personal responsibilities. This makes finding study spaces at a convenient time important. Limited hours for the library can make it hard for students to use all of the library resources they have available. Changes in the library hours can make it challenging for students to understand which floors are available, study areas, and services offered. This can affect students during exam periods when they might need a particular time to study or use services offered by the library.

Unavailable seating has become more apparent when analyzing how frequently students leave the library due to not finding an available place to study. Figure 2 shows that 80.4% of participants reported leaving the library due to a lack of available seating. Additionally, 43.5% chose sometimes, 30.4% chose often, and 6.5% chose very often. The data shows that the shortage of seating is not just a small inconvenience but rather a problem for students using the library. Students who cannot locate open seating can be forced to relocate, reducing the time they have for their tasks. Figures 1 and 2 provide important insight into addressing seating availability and improving how students can locate open seating in the library.

# Temperature and Ventilation

The library's physical environment also presents challenges. Temperature control on certain floors fluctuates due to weather conditions in warmer months. These temperatures can make it hard for students to focus and study for longer periods of time. The library’s poor, inconsistent temperatures, paired with subpar ventilation, affect students' ability to study, especially on a crowded floor with others. Students who depend on the library to study may have few alternatives when faced with these issues. These problems make it difficult for students to study and remain productive in longer study sessions.

# Study Room Reservations
Reserving study rooms can be challenging for students who study in groups or collaborate on projects. During busy time periods, these group study locations can be difficult to find available. Students might have trouble finding an available location to study in a group, with conflicting schedules reducing group productivity. Limited study room availability can make it difficult for students to secure private spaces for presentations, discussions, and group projects.

The issues presented show how overcrowding, operating hours, uncomfortable temperatures, and difficulties with reserving study rooms can negatively affect students at the UCSB Library. These problems are important to address during the academic period and rely on resources being available and students being comfortable. Understanding these problems is important to determine how effectively the UCSB Library meets students' needs.

Survey context: These results represent responses from 46 UCSB students and should be understood as feedback from this sample, rather than a statistically representative estimate of the entire student population.


## 4. Similar Problems at Other Universities {#similarproblems}

These challenges that we are seeing are not unique to UCSB, as students at other universities have reported similar concerns regarding study-space availability, operating hours, comfortable study spaces, and access to library resources. Research conducted at these institutions demonstrates how such issues can affect students' experience and provides insight into these problems.

# Study Space Availability
At the University of Central Florida (UCF), Allen and Moyer (2021) examined how students used different types of furniture in the university's main library. At the time, the library had approximately 1,903 available seats for 38,600 full-time-equivalent students, providing seating for only 4.9% of that population at once.

To take a deeper look at the student preferences, researchers completed 30 walkthroughs between July and October of 2018, taking note of what seats were occupied. The results showed that tables with dividers had a 59% utilization rate compared to 31% for tables without dividers.

-- insert the image here--

Researchers also saw that students would prefer to work in an open area even when study rooms were available. This shows that students' seating preferences depend not only on availability but also on the type of study environment.

These findings are relevant to UCSB because they show that the challenge is not simply the number of seats available. The organization and design of what is available can also impact how efficiently students make use of the library. By understanding students' preferences, UCSB can evaluate whether its current seating arrangement and layout meet the needs of its students.

# Limited Library Hours
Another challenge students face is the limited library operating hours. At East Carolina University, Harmon and Sewell (2024) examined how extending weekend hours affected the usage of the Laupus Health Sciences Library.

In response to students' requests, the university changed its budget to extend weekend operating hours by ten hours per week. These hours were divided by adding three hours on Friday, three hours on Saturday, and four hours on Sunday. Researchers compared library usage during the year before and after the changes, excluding examination periods. Their findings showed that students arrived earlier, stayed later, and overall library usage increased across the study.

During the extended operating hours, 8% of Friday entries, 11% of Saturday entries, and 21% of Sunday entries occurred during the newly added time periods. The total number of library items checked out also increased by 40.18% during non-exam periods.

The same study found that between 15.91% and 17.73% of reserved study-room hours occurred during the newly added hours, depending on the semester. This demonstrates that students were using dedicated study spaces, not only entering the building.

-- insert the image here--

We see through this study how extending library hours could help accommodate students with different schedules and study needs. For UCSB, this research provides support for evaluating whether additional evening and weekend hours could improve access to study spaces.

# Limited Library Hours
Beyond these improvements, we can also see how some universities have implemented digital applications to make library resources more accessible. At University College London (UCL), Izmir Tunahan and Altamirano (2022) examined library seating patterns using occupancy sensors installed beneath the desks.

UCL introduced technology to monitor approximately 4,000 study spaces across its libraries, collecting occupancy information at 10-minute intervals. This information was incorporated into the UCL Go app, allowing students to view available study spaces in real time rather than searching through the library themselves.

The researchers also examined how student seating preferences changed before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. For example, average library utilization in February 2019 was 58.4%, compared to 38.0% in February 2022. The study also found that before the pandemic, students generally preferred seating with natural lighting, outdoor views, and privacy. After the pandemic, students appeared to place greater importance on having distance between themselves and others.

-- insert the image here--
These findings demonstrate how occupancy data can help universities understand which spaces students prefer and how those preferences change over time. The technology also provides students with information that can help them plan library visits and locate available seating.

At UCSB, the existing Waitz system already provides broad occupancy information; research from UCL supports evaluating whether additional seat-level information could make that information more useful when combined with study room reservations and operating hours in one application.

All in all, these studies demonstrate how other universities have addressed similar challenges involving study space, operating hours, and access to library information. The research provides a foundation for evaluating improvements at UCSB, particularly in making better use of existing seating, providing more flexible study hours, and improving access to library resources through technology. ## 6. Call to Action {#calltoaction}

We hope that this information inspires immediate action among Isla Vista residents. We can't wait for the zombies to arrive before we act. Mathematical models of an outbreak suggest that only quick, aggressive action can prevent the collapse of society once zombies appear [2]. Every month of delay makes the walls more expensive and the hordes harder to stop. We urge the district's Board of Directors to adopt the four-step plan before the end of the year.

### Appendix A. Survey Methodology {#appendix-a}

The survey was conducted using the ZAS (Zombie Apocalypse Survey), a widely-used national scale devised by Kenneth Smith. The ZAS asks respondents to rate their agreement with a series of statements about zombie survival on a five-point scale, from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree." We recruited 173 undergraduate students on and around the UCSB campus, and all responses were anonymous.
